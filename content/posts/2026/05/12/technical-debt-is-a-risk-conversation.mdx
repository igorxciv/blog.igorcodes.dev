An engineer says, "The payment provider code is tightly coupled and hard to test." A manager hears that the code is ugly and engineering would like time to make it nicer. Product hears that the roadmap is about to get more expensive, for reasons nobody has explained.

Everyone heard the same sentence, and the debt behind it is real. But the engineer left the translation to the rest of the room, and nobody in a planning meeting wants to work out what tight coupling means for the next release. <mark>That translation gap is where good technical debt proposals go to die.</mark>

This is the format I use to close it.

<KeyPoints>

- Technical debt is worth raising when it changes the cost of future work, and the proposal has to show that cost.
- A decision-ready proposal has a one-sentence problem, a concrete impact and a bounded request with a way to check the result.
- A small proposal with a measurable finish line is easier to believe than an open-ended refactor.
- Name what the work delays, and change the framing for each audience while keeping the facts fixed.

</KeyPoints>

## Debt is fine until it changes the cost of future work

Technical debt isn't automatically bad. If a team is still finding out whether a feature should exist, a fast and slightly awkward implementation is the responsible choice. Spending two extra weeks polishing the architecture of a feature nobody uses buys you expensive decoration.

<mark>Debt becomes a problem when it changes the economics of future work.</mark> You usually see it in ordinary symptoms:

- a two-day feature starts taking two weeks
- every release needs manual checks because nobody trusts the automated tests
- one vendor integration leaks into unrelated parts of the system
- engineers avoid a module because nobody understands its side effects
- estimates turn random, so product can't plan

At that point the technical issue has become a planning issue, and planning issues are business issues. In the billing example I use through this post, the debt sits in one place and the risk spreads out from it.

## Managers don't prioritize clean code

That can feel frustrating, but it's healthy. A manager's job is to decide priorities under constraints, and a beautiful codebase isn't one of the constraints. They're thinking about the next quarter, release risk, team health and what saying yes to one thing costs everything else.

So when an engineer says "this module has high coupling and weak test coverage," the manager has to work out the rest alone. Does it delay a release, raise production risk or burn the team out?

That translation is our job. Good managers can follow engineering detail, but a prioritization conversation shouldn't open with a reverse-engineering exercise. The more decision-ready the proposal, the more the conversation turns to trade-offs instead of taste.

## Use the smallest useful framework

The most useful format I know is boring.

<Steps title="A decision-ready technical debt proposal">

1. Problem. Name the technical issue in one sentence.
2. Impact. Connect it to users, delivery or team health.
3. Proposal. Ask for a specific investment with a scope and a way to check the result.

</Steps>

Remembering three words is easy. The hard part is refusing to hide behind engineering vocabulary when the decision is about risk.

### Name the problem in one sentence

The problem should be small enough to hold in your head. Here is the same issue written for an architecture review and for a planning meeting.

<VisualGrid columns="2">
  <VisualCard eyebrow="Too much" title="The architecture review" tone="warning">
    "The provider integration violates separation of concerns, creates tight coupling between billing, authentication, onboarding and plan management, and makes tests hard to write because provider logic is spread across multiple services."
  </VisualCard>
  <VisualCard eyebrow="Better" title="The planning version" tone="accent">
    "Our payment provider logic is spread across billing, onboarding and plan management, so every plan change means edits in several unrelated places."
  </VisualCard>
</VisualGrid>

The first might be accurate, but it's too much for a planning conversation. The second gives people a handle, and you can see the risk without knowing what separation of concerns means.

### Show what the architecture costs

This is where engineers often lose the room. We stop at "the architecture is bad," but bad architecture is the cause. The impact is what it does to users, the business or the team, and it gets stronger the more concrete you make it.

<VisualGrid columns="2">
  <VisualCard eyebrow="Delivery" title="Make time visible" tone="accent">
    "Last quarter this added about six weeks of extra work across three billing changes."
  </VisualCard>
  <VisualCard eyebrow="Release risk" title="Name the confidence problem">
    "Every plan change needs manual regression testing because the automated tests miss provider edge cases."
  </VisualCard>
  <VisualCard eyebrow="Roadmap" title="Tie it to an upcoming goal">
    "The enterprise plan could slip, because SSO and billing both depend on this module."
  </VisualCard>
  <VisualCard eyebrow="Team health" title="Surface knowledge debt">
    "Only two engineers are comfortable changing this flow. If either is away, delivery stalls."
  </VisualCard>
</VisualGrid>

None of these hide the technical problem. They tie it to something the organization already knows how to weigh.

### Ask for something with edges

A weak proposal sounds like "let's think about what to do with this." That hands someone else homework. A stronger one sounds like this:

> I propose we spend one sprint isolating the payment provider behind a small internal interface. After that, the next plan iteration should take less than a week. We can check this on the upcoming SSO plan change.

It's easier to evaluate because it has edges:

- **Scope.** One sprint.
- **Action.** Isolate the provider.
- **Expected result.** The next plan change takes under a week.
- **Validation.** Measure it on the SSO change.

The manager may still say no, and that's fine. You're now discussing whether one sprint is worth removing a specific delivery risk, which beats arguing about whether clean code matters.

## Same facts, different framing

Product, engineering managers and tech leads each care about a different kind of risk. Keep the facts the same and change the framing.

With a tech lead you can go deep on coupling and testability, as long as you still tie them to impact. Matching the explanation to the decision someone has to make is ordinary communication, and you already do it elsewhere. If I explain a slow page to a user, I don't start with B-trees. I say the page is slow because the system has to scan too much data.

## A billing proposal, before and after

Imagine a billing system where payment provider logic has leaked everywhere. The engineer version might sound like this:

> The billing code is too coupled to Stripe. We need to abstract it because it is hard to test and hard to add new plan types.

That's not terrible, but it still asks the reader to do the translation. Here is a version you can take into planning.

<Brief title="Decision-ready version">

- **Problem.** Payment provider logic is spread across billing, onboarding and plan management, so every plan change means edits in several places.
- **Impact.** The last two plan changes took about three extra weeks because we had to test upgrades, downgrades and failed payments by hand. That puts next quarter's enterprise plan release at risk.
- **Proposal.** Spend one sprint isolating provider logic behind one internal billing interface, and measure it on the SSO plan change. If that change takes less than a week and needs no manual checks across unrelated flows, the work paid off.

</Brief>

Now people can argue about the trade-off itself. Is one sprint worth this risk? Do we believe the estimate? Could a smaller version work, and what happens if we wait? Those are the right questions, and the engineer version never got anyone to them.

## Make the proposal small enough to believe

"Let's refactor the billing system" is usually a fog machine dressed up as a proposal. It sounds expensive and open-ended, the kind of project that starts with optimism and ends with everyone crying into Jira tickets. A believable proposal breaks it into pieces small enough to schedule.

<Compare title="From vague refactor to bounded risk reduction">

<CompareColumn title="Before" muted>

1. "Let's refactor the billing system"
2. Scope unknown
3. Cost unknown
4. No finish line

</CompareColumn>

<CompareColumn title="After">

1. One sprint to isolate one provider
2. Two days of tests on the riskiest checkout flows
3. One week to remove duplicate plan calculation logic
4. Done when the next plan change takes under a week

</CompareColumn>

</Compare>

Nobody knows when "make the architecture better" is done. <mark>The goal is to reduce one specific risk and to know when you have.</mark> If the finish line is "the next plan change takes under a week," you can measure it. Hit it or miss it, you learn something about the system.

## Name what the work delays

If we ask for one sprint, that sprint might delay a feature, slow short-term velocity or need help from QA. A credible proposal says so up front:

> This will likely delay the small admin reporting improvement by one sprint. I think it's worth it because the enterprise plan depends on the billing flow, and fixing this now lowers the release risk for that work.

<PullQuote>

Technical debt work is still work. The sprint you ask for comes out of someone else's plan.

</PullQuote>

Nobody in the room is asking whether quality matters. They're asking whether this is the right quality investment right now, and sometimes the answer is no. If the feature may be deleted next month, maybe don't refactor it. If five internal users touch the system and it changes twice a year, the ugly code may be fine.

A hard regulatory deadline can push real debt down the list too. <mark>Good engineering includes knowing when not to polish.</mark>

## Knowledge debt is delivery risk too

Some debt lives in people's heads. If only one engineer understands a critical system, the company carries a risk, and that engineer usually did nothing wrong. Often they solve problems so fast that nobody else ever has to learn the area. When they're away, the team slows down or stops.

That's a system problem, so the best proposal is sometimes something other than "give me time to fix this myself":

> I propose that another engineer implements this cleanup while I supervise and review the design. That removes the single-person dependency and gives the team more coverage in this area.

You fix the code and spread the knowledge in the same sprint. Delegating debt work is a senior engineering skill because it lowers operational risk.

## Try it on one problem this week

Take one painful technical problem from your current project and write it in this format:

```text label="Template"
Problem: [one sentence]
Impact: [what this means for users, delivery or team health]
Proposal: [specific request with scope, cost and expected result]
```

Then read it back and ask whether someone else would understand why the team should do it. Most first drafts only explain what we want to do. If you're not sure, show it to a teammate and ask whether the why is clear. The first draft will probably be clumsy, and that's fine. You're practising translation, and nobody expects a polished business case.

## Before you ask for the sprint

None of this guarantees approval. It does change what people are deciding.

<Checklist title="Before you send the proposal">

- Lead with the problem in one sentence, without implementation details.
- Connect it to users, delivery or team health.
- Make a concrete request.
- Keep the scope small enough to believe.
- Say how you'll check the result.
- Say what work gets delayed.
- Change the framing for the audience and keep the facts the same.

</Checklist>

<Takeaway>

Technical debt work starts before the refactor, when you make the risk clear enough for someone else to decide. A request for one sprint to reduce a specific delivery risk gets a real answer. A request for time to clean code rarely does.

</Takeaway>
